Fast growing intracellular Mycobacterium tuberculosis populations evade antibiotic treatment

Abstract

Slow growth rates are a hallmark of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), one of the deadliest human pathogens. Arrested growth of Mtb has been associated with antibiotic tolerance and the ability to persist in the host. A physiologically relevant subpopulation of Mtb localizes within host macrophages, although how this intracellular residence in heterogeneous host cells affect Mtb growth remains elusive. We developed a robust, high-content, live, single-cell imaging approach to track infected human macrophage populations with high spatiotemporal resolution. We discovered the presence of fast-growing intracellular Mtb populations with doubling times below 16 hours. These fast-growing subpopulations were more likely to evade the first-line antibiotics rifampicin, isoniazid and pyrazinamide when compared to the slow-growing populations. Our data identifies fast-growing intracellular Mtb as a mechanism of antibiotic evasion.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) has been one of the most devasting infectious diseases throughout human history, affecting humanity for over 15,000 years (1) and resulting in 1.25 million deaths in 2023 (2). The current chemotherapy regimen for TB requires at least six months and four antibiotics to cure the disease. The reasons behind this prolonged treatment duration and multi-drug requirements are multifactorial and remain poorly understood (3-5).

Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), the causative agent of TB, can adopt an intracellular lifestyle and replicate within host cells during infection (6). Compelling evidence indicates a host cell contribution to antibiotic accumulation and efficacy (7-11), but the underlying mechanisms of these pharmacokinetic/dynamic interactions remain elusive. Further to that, Mtb will encounter a diverse set of both intracellular and extracellular microenvironments that lead to single-cell phenotypic variability (12, 13). However, the relationship between the host and pathogen, the subsequent single-cell heterogeneity and resultant antimicrobial susceptibility are largely undefined.

Antibiotic efficacy has been primarily determined using in vitro bulk assays that measure conventional parameters such as minimal inhibitory and minimal bactericidal concentrations (14). However, the use of such planktonic liquid cultures does not address single-cell heterogeneity and also precludes any host cell contribution towards antibiotic efficacy (15). Single-cell image-based in vitro studies of antibiotic treatment responses that overcome bulk determinations are especially important to address the highly heterogenous nature of Mtb infection (16, 17), but they too do not mimic intracellular environments. Single-cell image-based in cellulo studies overcome bulk determinations and mimic physiological intracellular environments (18-21), but are often limited in the spatial and temporal resolution necessary to fully capture the heterogeneity of intracellular Mtb response to antibiotics.

To address these challenges, we designed an image-based approach to monitor Mtb intracellular replication at the single-macrophage level, with high spatiotemporal resolution capable of tracking infection initialization to endpoint. Using this approach, we discovered that a subpopulation of infected human macrophages harbored fast-growing Mtb populations. These fast-growing intracellular Mtb populations evade antibiotic treatment, likely representing a phenotypically and clinically relevant population of bacteria during TB chemotherapy.

Discussion

We show that Mtb has the facultative capacity to adopt a remarkably wide range of intracellular growth phenotypes. Whilst other approaches have previously described such heterogeneity in vitro, in cellulo and in vivo (9, 16, 17, 21, 29, 30), our approach is capable of tracking individual infected macrophage populations and quantifying the intracellular Mtb growth dynamics. By focusing on intracellular Mtb populations that doubled, we yielded a metric that was comparable to previous bulk assessments of Mtb growth rates, as well as selecting for the host macrophages/intracellular Mtb populations that are the most permissive/proliferative and subsequently the most problematic when it comes to successfully treating infection.

The macrophages harboring fast-growing Mtb are likely to be relevant during the infection as Mtb permissive macrophages have been reported in human TB (6) and mouse models of TB (29, 31). Thus, our data potentially explain the biphasic behavior of certain antibiotics in mouse models of TB, such as PZA (32). Our results also agree with the observed intracellular growth of Mtb in animal models where the logarithmic increase of bacteria does not always match a slow growth rate (32).

Antibiotic phenotypic tolerance or persistence is conventionally associated with the slow (or non) growth behavior of Mtb and other bacteria under host environmental stress (33). Unexpectedly, we show that in the time frame of our experiments, fast-growing Mtb evades antibiotic action likely without developing resistance mutations and mostly owing to phenotypic changes triggered by the intracellular milieu (34, 35). We identified here an additional mechanism by which fast-growing intracellular Mtb evades antibiotic treatment. Our data agrees with reports in E. coli, where fast-growing resilience has been postulated as a mechanism of antibiotic escape in vitro (36).

Several scenarios could account for our findings. One possibility is that either macrophages or rapidly replicating Mtb fail to internalize effective concentrations of antibiotics (36). Alternatively, infection may enhance the efflux capacity of the host macrophages or intracellular Mtb, subsequently reducing antibiotic accumulation (37). Several mechanisms lead to RIF tolerance, heteroresistance, or persistence (38). In E. Coli, fast growing bacteria are more resistant to irreversibly-binding, ribosome-targeting antibiotics such as streptomycin and kanamycin (39).

Therefore, based on data presented here we propose a novel mechanism of antibiotic evasion in intracellular Mtb, where compounds targeting a canonically slow-growing infection are being evaded by a previously unknown fast-growing intracellular phenotype. Our findings have potential implications for TB chemotherapy. These observations raise the possibility that some anti-TB antibiotics may fail to target fast-growing populations effectively or that these fast-growing populations evade antibiotic treatment. Further exploration of the biology and resilience of these fast-growing intracellular populations could be important for designing improved TB treatment strategies.

Materials and Methods

M. tuberculosis culture

Fluorescent M. tuberculosis H37Rv pTEC19 WT and ΔRD1 strains were used in this study (7). Those strains were grown in Middlebrook 7H9 broth supplemented with 0.2% glycerol, 0.05% Tween-80, 10% ADC and 50mg/L Hygromycin B as a selection marker for fluorescent strains. Bacterial cultures were incubated at 37°C with rotation in 50mL conical tubes.

Human iPSC culture

KOLF2-C1 AAVS1:EGFP human iPSCs were a gift from the Skarnes Lab (The Jackson Laboratory, CT, USA) and were generated as previously described (22). Briefly, the KOLF2-C1 AAVS1 cells express a EGFP reporter under the control of the strong CAGG promoter in the safe harbor locus AAVS1. The cells were maintained in Vitronectin XF (StemCell Technologies, 100–0763)-coated plates with Essential 8 medium (Gibco, A1517001). Cells were authenticated by STR profiling and checked regularly for Mycoplasma contamination. Cells were passaged using Versene (Gibco, 15040066).

iPSDM differentiation

Monocyte factories were set up following a previously reported protocol (44). Embryoid bodies (EBs) were fed daily with two times 50% medium changes with E8 supplemented with 50ng ml−1 hBMP4 (Peprotech, 120-05), 50ng ml−1 hVEGF (Peprotech, 100-20) and 20ng ml−1 hSCF (Peprotech, 300-07) for 3 days. On day 4, the EBs were collected and seeded at 100–150 EBs per T175 or 250–300 per T225 flask in XVIVO-factory medium. These monocyte factories were fed weekly for 5 weeks until monocytes were observed in the supernatant. Up to 50% of the supernatant was collected weekly and centrifuged at 300g for 5 min. The cells were resuspended in XVIVO-differentiation medium. Monocytes were plated at 5 × 106 cells per 10cm Petri dish to differentiate over 7 days; on day 4, a 50% medium change was performed. To detach, cells were washed once with PBS (pH 7.4), then incubated with Versene for 15 min at 37 °C and 5% CO2 before diluting 1:3 with PBS and gently scraping. Macrophages were centrifuged at 300g and plated for experiments.

Infection of iPSDM with M. tuberculosis

Induced pluripotent stem cell derived macrophages (iPSDM) were seeded at a density of 50,000 cells per well of an olefin-bottomed 96-well plate (Phenoplate, 605532, Perkin-Elmer). Mid-logarithmic-phase bacterial cultures (OD600 0.5–1.0) were centrifuged at 2,000g for 5 min and washed twice in PBS. Pellets were then shaken vigorously for 1 min with 2.5–3.5 mm glass beads (VWR, 332124 G) and bacteria resuspended in 10 ml macrophage culture medium before being centrifuged at 300g for 5 min to remove large clumps. The top 7 ml of bacterial suspension was taken, the OD600 was measured, and the suspension was diluted appropriately for infection. After 2 h of uptake, extracellular bacteria were removed with two washes in PBS and macrophages were incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 24h. Then, media was changed with media containing or not antibiotics before proceeding to the subsequent live-cell imaging.

Antibiotic treatment

Antibiotic concentrations used in this study, defined as in cellulo EC50 and EC99, were previously determined in our group by measuring using in cellulo imaging (7, 45). The growth of E2Crimson-Mtb in iPSDM with various concentrations of Pyrazinamide (Sigma, PHR1576-500mg), Rifampicin (Sigma, R3501-1g) and Isoniazid (Sigma, I3377-5G). The values of EC50 for PZA, RIF and INH were respectively 60μg/mL, 0.1μg/mL and 0.04μg/mL. The values of EC99 for PZA, RIF and INH were respectively 400μg/mL, 2μg/mL and 2μg/mL. Antibiotics were added into the infected macrophages after 24 h of infection period.

Image acquisition

Image acquisition was achieved with the OPERA Phenix high-content, confocal, incubator microscope with a 40x water-immersion 1.1NA objective. A single image of 323µm x 323µm was acquired across 9 contiguous positions which were compiled together into a 3x3 mosaic of 904µm x 904µm with a 10% overlap between tiles. This tessellated field of view was repeated across 3x 2µm z-slices and the 2x green/red imaging channels. The channel acquisition parameters were λex = 488, λem = 522 (exposure time 100ms) for KOLF-EGFP macrophages and λex = 640, λem = 706 (exposure time 200ms) for E2Crimson-Mtb. This was then repeated every 30/60 minutes for 150/75 frames to reach a total timelapse acquisition of 75 hours. This process was repeated over 42 different imaging wells, each with a different Mtb strain or antibiotic condition within, resulting in a final acquisition of 349,72 images (2.58TB) per biological repeat. A total of 4 biological repeats were conducted. The untiled images were then exported out of the Harmony software (Revvity, version 4.9) to allow for an open-source Python-based image analysis.

Image analysis

The first step in the Python-based image analysis was to tile the images into full field-of-view mosaics. This facilitated the tracking of macrophages across individual tiles, greatly increasing the number of tracks. This tiling was achieved using a bespoke script, based on the DaskFusion repository (46), that extracts the Cartesian coordinates of each image tile from the OPERA Phenix metadata and stiches them together into a Dask image volume (47). This lazy-loading approach enabled the selective rendering of individual frames, z-slices, or cropped regions of interest, conserving computational power by avoiding the need to load the entire 62.95GB-per-condition image volume for each visual examination or partial analysis.

The next step in the analysis was to segment the cytoplasmic extent of the macrophages. Due to the inherent heterogeneity of macrophage morphology, we decided to train our own segmentation model using Cellpose 2 (48). We manually segmented 4934 individual cell segments across a range of 72 different images, representing a diverse range of time points, cellular densities, infection statuses and drug conditions. The resulting self-trained model improved the Jaccard index (a measure of the number of objects correctly identified) by 25% and the intersection over union (IoU, a measure of the accuracy of individual segments) by 2.2%. The increase in the Jaccard index metric highlights an improvement upon the primary limitation of using the pre-trained model, which would frequently over- or under-segment cells into merged- or multiply-fragmented instances. The marginal improvement of the IoU indicates that the boundary accuracy of individual segments was already robust, underscoring the quality of the Cellpose generalist approach.

After segmentation, each cell was localized and tracked using the btrack multi-object tracking algorithm (49). This localization step included the quantification of several cellular properties, most importantly the total intracellular Mtb content. This was defined as the area of E2-crimson fluorescence above a threshold pixel value (480) that was obtained through manual segmentation of Mtb using a custom napari (28) key-binding script. These cellular properties were then stored, along with the centroid and time coordinates of each segment, as Pytracklet objects that serve as the input to the tracking. The btrack algorithm utilizes a combination of a motion model and a visual feature comparison to predict the movement of each cell and compare the visual properties measured in the localization step across frames to ensure that each segment is accurately linked to its correct temporal evolution. The result of this is a time series of united objects that chart the trajectory of a single macrophage with associated properties, including intracellular Mtb burden.

Statistical analysis

The final step in the image analysis pipeline was to process the time-series information of intracellular Mtb content for each cell into growth curves, from which single-cell intracellular doubling amounts and times could be extracted. This firstly involves a linear interpolation of the data to account for points at which the segmentation may have been missed or image frame skipped. A nonparametric locally weighted scatterplot smoothing (LOWESS) model (50) was then applied to this time series to generate a smooth, continuous curve. This allowed for a reliable estimation of intracellular Mtb doubling times as it removes any fluctuations from the data that could result in an inaccurate assessment of population doubling.

Two types of statistical test were applied to the populations of doubling times. Firstly, to assess the overall differences in distributions between the control, untreated doubling times and the antibiotic treated populations, a Mann-Whitney U test was conducted. This test was chosen as the populations of doubling times for each condition did not meet the requirements of normal distribution as verified by a Shapiro-Wilk test. The results of the Mann-Whitney U tests are shown in Table S1. Secondly, to qualify the differences in the subpopulations of fast, normal and slow doubling times a Chi-Squared statistic (χ2) was calculated. This test was appropriately chosen because it measures the discrepancies between observed and expected counts for each subpopulation, meaning that a significant p-value indicates that the differences in proportions are unlikely to have occurred by chance. The results of these tests are shown in Table S2. All statistical tests were conducted using Python.

Visualization

Visualization of timelapse imagery, accompanying segmentation and tracked macrophage populations were all conducted within napari, a multi-dimensional image viewer for Python (28). Cropped video glimpses of macrophages and Mtb were compiled as RGB .mp4 video files to facilitate the iterative loading and classification of macrophage phenotypes using napari key-bindings.
